01 · Ethics · 1,900 words

Pack It In, Pack It Out: Keeping Colorado Colorado

The state is growing. The mountains are not. Here is what that math actually asks of you.

ZW Zach WennstedtFounder & Editor-in-Chief · Raised in Boulder, Colorado

What does Leave No Trace mean in Colorado?

Leave No Trace means you leave a site with no evidence you were there. In Colorado that comes down to seven habits: plan ahead, stay on durable surfaces, pack out every piece of trash including food scraps, leave rocks and antlers where they are, kill your fire until it is cold to the touch, watch wildlife from a distance, and yield the trail. The organization that wrote those principles is headquartered in Boulder.

I grew up in Boulder. That single fact shapes everything I think about this subject, because Boulder decided what kind of town it wanted to be before most people were paying attention. In 1967 the city passed the first open space sales tax in the United States — residents voted to tax themselves to buy land and then not build on it. That program now protects tens of thousands of acres of foothills, grassland and trail. It is the reason you can still stand at the base of the Flatirons and see a mountain instead of a subdivision.

I have watched the pressure on that land change over my lifetime. Colorado had roughly 2.2 million people in 1970. It has close to six million now. Nearly three times the people, using the same finite number of trailheads, campsites, alpine lakes and dirt pullouts. Nobody added mountains.

That is not a complaint about newcomers. I would rather live in a state people want to move to. But it does mean the old casual habits do not scale anymore. When a hundred people a year visited a spot, a little sloppiness disappeared into the landscape. When ten thousand people a year visit it, the same sloppiness becomes a landfill.

The Extra Bag

Here is the one habit I would hand to every person who camps in this state. It costs nothing and it takes about ten minutes.

I bring two trash bags. One for my garbage. One for everybody else's.

Before I break camp, I walk the site and the fifty yards around it, and I fill the second bag. Bottle caps. Cigarette butts. Half a tent stake. Foil that somebody thought would burn. Wet wipes, which never break down and are somehow always there. Fishing line. A sock. Broken glass from a fire ring, which is the worst of it, because glass in a fire ring means the next person's dog gets cut.

Then it goes in the truck and out at the first dumpster I pass.

I want to be clear that this is not a heroic act. It is ten minutes and a two-cent bag. The reason I keep doing it is that the effect compounds in a way that is genuinely surprising. Litter attracts litter — a clean site stays clean, and a site with three beer cans in the weeds will have thirty by August. If you strip a site back to zero, you have not just cleaned it once. You have reset the baseline for everyone who camps there for the rest of the season.

Leave it better than you found it is not a slogan. It is a maintenance schedule, and every one of us is on the crew.

The Seven Principles, Translated For Actual Colorado

The Leave No Trace Center for Outdoor Ethics publishes seven principles. They are good. They are also written to apply to every landscape in the country, which makes them a little abstract. Here is what each one means at 9,000 feet in a dry year.

1. Plan ahead and prepare

In Colorado this mostly means: check the fire ban before you leave the house. Bans change by county and sometimes by week, and they get stricter fast in a drought year. Also check whether your spot needs a reservation. A lot of Colorado is now permitted or timed, and finding that out at the gate is a wasted day.

2. Travel and camp on durable surfaces

Use the site that already exists. Colorado's alpine tundra above treeline is the fragile part — those tiny cushion plants can be decades old and a single boot print off-trail can last years. Below treeline, camp on dirt, rock or dry grass, and stay at least 200 feet from lakes and streams. On national forest land, dispersed camping is generally capped at 14 days within a 28-day period, and you are expected to move on after that.

3. Dispose of waste properly

Pack out all trash. All of it. Orange peels and apple cores are trash — they take months to break down at altitude and they teach animals to associate humans with food. For human waste, dig a cathole six to eight inches deep, at least 200 feet from water, trail and camp, and pack out the paper. Some high-use areas and all technical routes now require a wag bag. Carry one anyway.

4. Leave what you find

Rocks, wildflowers, shed antlers, old mining artifacts — leave them. Colorado is full of historic mining structures and it is genuinely illegal to remove artifacts from many of them. The columbine is also protected: picking one on public land is against state law.

5. Minimize campfire impacts

This is the one that burns down the state. More on it below.

6. Respect wildlife

Never feed anything. A fed animal is a dead animal, and in Colorado that includes bears, which get relocated once and euthanized the second time. Store food in a hard-sided vehicle or a bear canister. During the September and October elk rut, stay well back — a bull elk in rut is a 700-pound animal with a bad attitude and antlers, and people get hurt every single year taking photos.

7. Be considerate of other visitors

Uphill hiker has the right of way. Bikes yield to hikers, everyone yields to horses. Keep the music in your headphones. People drove three hours to hear wind in the pines, not your speaker.

Kill The Fire. Then Kill It Again.

The overwhelming majority of Colorado wildfires are started by people. Not lightning. Us. Abandoned campfires, chain drag from a trailer, a hot exhaust in dry grass, fireworks, a cigarette out a window.

The campfire ones are the most preventable and the most infuriating, because the procedure is not complicated.

  • Let it burn down. Stop adding wood an hour before you want to sleep. You want ash, not logs.
  • Drown it. Not a splash. Pour water until the hissing stops completely.
  • Stir it. Get a stick or a shovel in there and turn everything over. Scrape the coals off the unburned wood. Find the embers hiding underneath.
  • Drown it again. Every surface you just exposed needs water too.
  • Touch it. Back of your hand, an inch above the ash. If you feel heat, it is not out. Repeat until it is cold.

If you do not have enough water to do all of that, you do not have enough water to have a fire. That is the whole rule. Dirt alone does not work — it insulates coals and they can smolder underground for days, then surface into dry grass after you have driven home.

And in a year like this one, the honest answer is often to skip the fire entirely. A camp stove boils water faster, does not require a permit, and cannot start a 100,000-acre fire. I like a campfire as much as anyone. I have also spent enough Augusts watching smoke columns from my driveway to have gotten comfortable going without.

What Changed, And What Did Not

The thing I would tell somebody who just moved here is that Colorado is not actually fragile in the way people mean when they say it. It is a hard, high, dry place that has survived mining booms, clearcuts and a century of bad decisions. It is tougher than we are.

What is fragile is the experience. The reason you drove up here. The silence, the water you can put your hand in, the site that looks like nobody has ever been there. That is the part that degrades first, and that is the part that a single bag of somebody else's garbage can restore.

Colorado's population tripled in my lifetime and the mountains did not get bigger. So the only variable left is how carefully each of us moves through them. That is not a burden. It is ten minutes, one extra bag, and a fire you touched with your hand before you left.

Do that and this state will still be worth driving to in another 150 years.

Where do I find a legal campsite in Colorado?

Start with dispersed camping on national forest land, which is free and legal on most Forest Service roads, or reserve a developed site through the managing agency. Our sister guide at Colorado Camping Spots maps out where to go by region, and the Colorado Parks & Wildlife and U.S. Forest Service sites in the resources section below list current closures and fire restrictions.

02 · History · 1,850 words

Colorado At 150: What A Century And A Half Actually Built

Gold, silver, railroads, skis and satellites — and one word nobody else in America gets to use this year.

ZW Zach WennstedtFounder & Editor-in-Chief · Raised in Boulder, Colorado

Why is Colorado called the Centennial State?

Colorado became the 38th state on August 1, 1876 — exactly 100 years after the Declaration of Independence. President Ulysses S. Grant signed the statehood proclamation, and the timing gave Colorado its nickname. That coincidence is also why 2026 is unusual: Colorado turns 150 in the same year the country turns 250.

History Colorado has a word for it. Sesquisemiquincentennial. It is not elegant and it will not fit on a t-shirt, but it is accurate, and Colorado is the only state in the country that gets to use it. Every other state's anniversary is just an anniversary. Ours is permanently stapled to the nation's, because of a hundred-year gap that a territorial legislature was very deliberately racing to hit.

So what did a century and a half actually produce? Here is the honest version, including the parts the tourism copy skips.

1858–1876: The Rush That Mostly Failed

Colorado begins, in the American sense, with a rumor. In 1858 prospectors found small amounts of gold near the mouth of Cherry Creek, where Denver sits now. The news traveled east and got wildly exaggerated on the way. An estimated hundred thousand people set out for the Rockies under the slogan "Pikes Peak or Bust."

Roughly half of them turned around. The gold was real but thin, and the ones who stayed mostly did not get rich. The people who did get rich sold them shovels, flour and lumber. That pattern — the boom enriching the supplier rather than the digger — repeats through the entire state's history, right up through the ski industry and the tech corridor.

Colorado Territory was organized in 1861. Statehood took another fifteen years and two failed attempts, and it finally arrived on August 1, 1876. The proclamation Grant signed is now held by History Colorado and was displayed publicly in Denver on the 150th anniversary this August.

1878–1893: Silver, And Then No Silver

The gold rush was the prologue. Silver was the fortune.

Leadville sits at 10,152 feet and is the highest incorporated city in the United States. In the late 1870s it became one of the richest square miles on the continent. The silver boom built opera houses, mansions, narrow-gauge railroads and an entire political class. Horace Tabor went from running a general store to serving in the U.S. Senate.

It ended almost overnight. In 1893 Congress repealed the Sherman Silver Purchase Act, the price of silver collapsed, and Colorado's economy went with it. Mines closed in weeks. Towns that had ten thousand residents had a few hundred within a decade. Most of Colorado's famous ghost towns are silver towns, not gold towns, and 1893 is the year that killed them.

The same year, something else happened that Colorado should be far better known for. In November 1893, Colorado's male voters approved women's suffrage by popular referendum. Wyoming had granted it earlier as a territory, but Colorado was the first state where men actually voted at the ballot box to extend the franchise to women — twenty-seven years before the Nineteenth Amendment.

1900–1945: Labor, Dust And A War Bargain

The mining economy that built Colorado also produced some of the ugliest labor conflict in American history. In April 1914, during a coal strike in Las Animas County, a confrontation between strikers and the Colorado National Guard ended with roughly two dozen people dead, including women and children who suffocated in a cellar beneath a burning tent colony. The Ludlow Massacre changed federal labor policy and it belongs in any honest telling of this state.

Then the plains. Eastern Colorado is not mountains, and in the 1930s the Dust Bowl hit Baca and Prowers counties as hard as anywhere in the country. Whole counties depopulated. The eastern third of Colorado has never fully recovered its population, and it remains the part of the state that visitors and marketers both routinely forget exists.

The turn came from the war. In 1942 the Army built Camp Hale near Leadville to train the 10th Mountain Division in alpine and ski warfare. Thousands of soldiers learned to ski at altitude in Colorado. When they came home, a remarkable number of them went into the ski business.

1946–1976: The Mountains Become A Product

Aspen reopened as a ski resort in 1946, backed by Walter Paepcke, who wanted a town that combined skiing with music, philosophy and design — an idea that sounded absurd and turned out to be worth billions.

Vail followed in 1962, founded by Pete Seibert and Earl Eaton, both 10th Mountain veterans. Within twenty years Colorado had converted its high country from a mining economy into a recreation economy. The mines had extracted metal. The resorts extracted altitude, snow and scenery, and unlike ore, those do not run out.

Then Colorado did something no place has done before or since. Denver was awarded the 1976 Winter Olympics. In 1972, Colorado voters passed a ballot measure barring the state from funding them. The Games were handed to Innsbruck. Colorado remains the only place ever to be awarded a modern Olympics and then turn them down.

That vote is the most Colorado thing in this entire article. It was driven by a coalition worried about growth, cost and environmental damage — the same argument the state is still having, fifty years later, in every mountain town council meeting.

1976–Now: Satellites, Labs And The Water Question

The modern Colorado economy is less photogenic and much larger. Boulder hosts a dense cluster of federal science — NOAA, NIST and NCAR among them — and the physics work done in those labs has produced multiple Nobel Prizes. Colorado Springs and the Front Range host a national aerospace and defense corridor that quietly employs tens of thousands.

And underneath all of it sits water. Colorado is a headwaters state. No major river flows into Colorado; they all start here and flow out. The Colorado River rises in Rocky Mountain National Park and ends up watering Phoenix, Los Angeles and the Imperial Valley. The 1922 Colorado River Compact divided that water among seven states based on flow measurements taken during an unusually wet stretch, and every water fight in the American West since has been an argument with that document.

Which is why a low snow year here is national news. See the water year piece below for what 2026 in particular is doing.

What The 150th Actually Looks Like

The commemoration is running all year and it is deliberately decentralized. The signature program is Stories in the Sky, a series of roughly 150 community drone shows staged across the state from late 2025 through December 2026. History Colorado is also running a digital passport designed to route people into small towns and independent businesses rather than the same overloaded trailheads.

On Colorado Day this August, the Capitol hosted a reenactment of the 1876 statehood proclamation, a cake cutting, a square dance inside the building, and lowriders parked on the west lawn — which is a fairly complete portrait of the state in one photograph.

There was also a small, genuinely nerdy detail worth noting. The Mile High marker on the Capitol steps was updated during the ceremony, but only temporarily. The official elevation datum is being recalculated by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and will not be finalized until 2027. Denver's exact mile-high line is, at this moment, provisional.

One hundred and fifty years in, and we are still measuring the place.

03 · Safety · 1,850 words

Reading A Colorado Mountain Day Before It Reads You

Thin air, the two o'clock rule, and why the forecast on your phone quietly stops being true above treeline.

ZW Zach WennstedtFounder & Editor-in-Chief · Raised in Boulder, Colorado

Why is it harder to breathe in Colorado?

The percentage of oxygen in the air never changes — it is about 20.9% everywhere. What changes is air pressure. At 10,000 feet the atmosphere pushes down with roughly 69% of the force it does at sea level, so each breath delivers about 69% as much oxygen. Denver, at 5,280 feet, is already down to about 82%. On a fourteener summit you are near 58%.

Colorado kills people with weather and altitude every year, and almost never dramatically. It is rarely an avalanche or a fall. It is far more often somebody who started a hike at ten in the morning, felt fine, kept going, and got caught above treeline at two in the afternoon in a lightning storm, dehydrated, with a headache they thought was from the drive.

All of that is predictable. Here is how to predict it.

Altitude is not a difficulty setting. It is a physics problem, and you can calculate exactly how much oxygen you are getting.

Altitude Sickness Is Not A Fitness Test

Acute mountain sickness does not care how strong you are. Marathon runners get it. Sedentary smokers sometimes do not. It is largely genetic lottery plus how fast you went up.

Symptoms usually start six to twelve hours after arriving high:

  • Headache that does not respond to ibuprofen
  • Nausea or loss of appetite
  • Fatigue far beyond the effort
  • Dizziness, and sleep that keeps breaking

The treatment for all of it is the same and it is not negotiable: go down. Descending a thousand feet fixes most cases within hours. Nothing else reliably does. Not rest, not aspirin, not pushing through.

The two dangerous escalations are high altitude cerebral edema and high altitude pulmonary edema. The tells are stumbling and confusion for the first, and a wet cough with breathlessness at rest for the second. Either one is a descend-immediately-and-call-911 situation. They are rare below 12,000 feet and they are not survivable by waiting.

Prevention that actually works:

  • Sleep low, climb high. Spend a night in Denver or Colorado Springs before you sleep in Leadville.
  • Drink more than feels necessary. Dry air pulls water out through your lungs at a rate you will not notice.
  • Skip the alcohol on night one. A beer at 9,000 feet does roughly what two do at sea level, and it wrecks the sleep you need to acclimate.
  • Give it 24 to 48 hours. Do the easy hike first. The fourteener is day three.

The Two O'Clock Rule

Here is the single most useful sentence in this article: be off the summit by noon and below treeline by two.

Colorado's summer weather runs on a daily engine. Morning sun heats the ground. Warm air rises up the slopes. By late morning it hits cold air aloft, condenses, and builds vertically into a thunderhead. By early afternoon that cell is producing lightning. The whole cycle is so reliable you can set a watch by it from roughly mid-June through mid-September.

This is why fourteener trailheads are full of cars at four in the morning. It is not machismo. It is arithmetic. A ten-mile round trip at altitude takes eight hours, and if you want to be below treeline at two, you started at five.

The North American Monsoon makes it worse. From roughly mid-July into early September, moisture pushes north from the Gulf of California and Colorado's afternoon storms stop being isolated and start being daily. Monsoon storms also arrive earlier — noon instead of two — and they dump enough water fast enough to cause flash flooding in narrow canyons and on burn scars.

If you hear thunder, count. Sound takes about five seconds to travel a mile. Under thirty seconds between flash and boom means the storm is close enough to hit you, and you should already be moving downhill and away from ridgelines, isolated trees and standing water. Wait thirty minutes after the last thunder before going back up.

Why Your Phone Forecast Is Wrong Above Treeline

The National Weather Service issues forecasts on a grid roughly 2.5 kilometers across. That is excellent resolution for a city. It is a blunt instrument in terrain where a 3,000-foot cliff, a valley floor and an exposed ridge can all fall inside one grid square.

Your weather app gives you the number for the nearest town. The nearest town is usually in a valley, several thousand feet below where you are going.

Two corrections you can do in your head:

  • Temperature. Subtract roughly 3 to 5 °F for every 1,000 feet you climb. An 80° day in Estes Park is a 50° day on Trail Ridge Road — before wind.
  • Wind. Ridges and passes routinely run two to three times the valley wind speed. A 15 mph forecast can be a 40 mph reality on an exposed saddle, and 40 mph wind at 45° is a hypothermia scenario in July.

Use the point forecast at weather.gov rather than a generic app, and drop the exact coordinates of your trailhead or summit into it. It will give you the grid square you are actually standing in, plus the forecast discussion, where a human meteorologist explains what they are uncertain about. That discussion is the most underused free resource in Colorado.

The Sun Is A Separate Problem

Ultraviolet radiation increases roughly 4 to 5 percent per 1,000 feet of elevation. At 12,000 feet you are getting somewhere near half again the UV you would get at sea level, and snow reflects most of what misses you.

People come home from Colorado with burns they did not think were possible in 45-degree weather. Sunscreen, real sunglasses and a brimmed hat are alpine safety equipment here, not vanity items. Snow blindness is a real and genuinely miserable condition and it happens to spring skiers every year.

Winter Has Its Own Rules

Two things matter above all others.

Chain law. CDOT activates traction and chain restrictions on I-70 and mountain passes during storms. Driving into an active restriction without proper tires can get you fined heavily, and the fine goes up sharply if you block the road. Check before you leave, not at the ramp.

Avalanche. Any time you leave a groomed run or travel on snow above treeline, you are in avalanche terrain. The Colorado Avalanche Information Center publishes a free daily forecast by zone. Read it. Colorado's snowpack has a persistent weak layer problem that makes it one of the most dangerous in North America, and low-snow years like this one tend to make that layer worse, not better.

The Whole Thing In Six Lines

  • Sleep a night low before you go high.
  • Start early. Summit by noon, below treeline by two.
  • Drink more water than you think you need.
  • Pull the point forecast for your actual coordinates, not the town.
  • Carry a layer, a headlamp and more food than the plan requires.
  • Pick a turnaround time before you start, and honor it even when you feel great.

That last one is the one people break. The mountain will still be there next weekend. Set the time, and go home.

04 · Conditions · 1,850 words

The 2026 Water Year: Why A Dry Winter Is Still Shaping Your Trip

Snowpack hit 61% of median in February — the worst reading in the modern record. Eight months later, that number is still deciding what you can do here.

ZW Zach WennstedtFounder & Editor-in-Chief · Raised in Boulder, Colorado

Is Colorado in a drought in 2026?

Yes. Colorado came out of the 2025–26 winter with statewide snowpack near 61% of median — ranked as the worst in a snow telemetry record that goes back to about 1987. Parts of Summit, Eagle, Pitkin, Lake and Park counties reached exceptional drought, the U.S. Drought Monitor's most severe category, and most of northwest Colorado sat in extreme or severe drought.

Almost nobody plans a Colorado trip around hydrology. You should, at least a little, because in this state the snow that fell in January is the reason a river is runnable in July, a campground has a fire ban in August, and the aspens turn a week and a half early in September.

Here is the chain, in plain terms, and where 2026 sits on it.

What A Water Year Even Is

Hydrologists do not use the calendar year. They use the water year, which runs October 1 through September 30. The logic is clean: the snow that starts falling in October is the water you will be drinking, floating and fighting fires with the following summer. Grouping October through September keeps one winter's snow with the summer it feeds.

The measurement that matters is not snow depth. It is snow water equivalent — how much actual water is locked in that snow. Two feet of dense wet snow holds far more water than four feet of the dry powder Colorado is famous for. A network of automated SNOTEL stations across the mountain West weighs the snowpack continuously and reports it as a percentage of the long-term median for that date.

That percentage is the single number that tells you what kind of summer Colorado is about to have.

What 2026 Read

Badly.

By mid-February the statewide figure was 61% of median. Officials noted that it would have taken record-breaking snowfall for the rest of the season just to reach a normal peak — and that did not happen. The state's fire agency said publicly that conditions in places looked worse than the winters that preceded 2012 and 2020, two of the most destructive fire years in Colorado history.

The effects showed up before the snow even melted. Frisco closed its Dillon Reservoir boat ramp for the entire 2026 season, on guidance that the reservoir would not fill enough to float the docks. That decision cost the town north of $300,000 in revenue, and it happened in February, based purely on what the snowpack was not doing.

The lack of snow also pushed fire risk earlier into the calendar and forced agencies to delay or cancel prescribed pile burns, which need snow on the ground to be conducted safely. That backlog of unburned fuel does not disappear. It waits.

Five Things It Changes For Visitors

1. Whitewater seasons compress

Colorado's rafting season is driven by runoff. In a good year, high water on the Arkansas, Colorado and Animas holds into early July and the big-water trips run for weeks. In a year like this, peak flow arrives earlier, drops faster, and the season for the high-consequence stretches can end in June.

That does not mean no rafting. It means outfitters shift to sections that run well at lower flow, and the trip you booked as a class IV may be running closer to class III. Call the outfitter and ask what the gauge is reading rather than what the brochure says. You can check the same gauges yourself — the U.S. Geological Survey publishes real-time streamflow for hundreds of Colorado sites, free.

2. Fishing gets restricted for the fish, not for you

Low flows mean warm water, and warm water holds less dissolved oxygen. When river temperatures climb into the upper 60s and low 70s, a trout that fights a long battle and gets released often dies anyway. Colorado Parks & Wildlife responds with voluntary fishing closures, usually asking anglers to stay off the water from late morning through evening.

These are voluntary. Honor them anyway. Fish early, fish the tailwaters below dams where the water comes out cold, and pack a thermometer. If it reads 70, go get breakfast.

3. Fire bans become the default

Expect restrictions, and expect them to change while you are already out there. The two tiers you will see most:

  • Stage 1 generally bans open fires outside permanent developed campground rings, plus fireworks and smoking outside a vehicle or cleared area. Liquid and gas stoves usually stay legal.
  • Stage 2 is far broader — typically no fires of any kind including charcoal, restrictions on chainsaws and welding, and limits on driving off established roads.

Bans are set county by county and agency by agency, so a single weekend trip can cross three different rule sets. Check each county you will be sleeping in, the day you leave.

4. Burn scars turn rain into flash floods

This is the one people underestimate. After a fire, soil can become hydrophobic — it sheds water instead of absorbing it. A short, ordinary thunderstorm over a burn scar can produce a debris flow in a drainage that has never flooded in living memory.

Colorado's 2025 season included the Lee Fire at roughly 137,758 acres, one of the largest in state history. Those scars will be generating flash flood risk for years. If a flash flood warning is issued for a burn area while you are camped in a canyon below it, leave. Do not wait to see water.

5. Fall color runs early and burns out faster

Drought-stressed aspens change sooner, hold less vivid color, and drop leaves faster than trees in a wet year. Guides that tell you to wait for the first week of October are repeating a normal-year rule.

For 2026, plan the high country — passes above 10,000 feet, the San Juans, Kebler Pass — for mid to late September. Mid-elevation spots like Kenosha and Guanella passes run late September into the first days of October. The Front Range and city parks hold latest, into mid-October. Color quality will vary sharply by microclimate: north-facing slopes and drainages with reliable water will look far better than dry exposed hillsides a mile away.

Why Colorado's Snow Is Everyone's Problem

Colorado is a headwaters state. Water flows out of here and essentially none flows in. The Colorado River starts in Rocky Mountain National Park and, by the time it is finished, has supplied cities and farmland across seven states and two countries.

The 1922 Colorado River Compact split that water using flow records from an unusually wet period, which means the river has been over-allocated on paper for most of a century. A 61% snow year in Colorado is therefore not a local inconvenience. It is a line item in reservoir operations from Lake Powell to the Imperial Valley.

The snow that falls on a pass in January is somebody's drinking water in July, in a city a thousand miles away. That is the actual reason Coloradans watch the SNOTEL numbers like a stock ticker.

The Honest Summary

2026 is a thin year. It does not mean cancel the trip. Colorado in a dry summer is still Colorado, and some of it is genuinely better — clearer skies for stargazing, firmer trails earlier in the season, and high routes that melt out weeks ahead of schedule.

It means shift the plan. Go earlier for water, later for color, and build in the assumption that you will be cooking on a stove rather than sitting around a fire. Check the county the morning you leave.

And if there is a burn scar above your campsite, look up before you unroll the sleeping bag.

05 · Climbing · 1,850 words

Boulders To Fourteeners: The Colorado Climbing Ladder

Four disciplines, two rating systems, 58 summits and one set of access rules people keep breaking.

ZW Zach WennstedtFounder & Editor-in-Chief · Raised in Boulder, Colorado

How many 14ers are in Colorado?

Fifty-eight, according to the Colorado Geological Survey. The highest is Mount Elbert at 14,440 feet, which is also the second-highest peak in the contiguous United States. The state also holds roughly 647 peaks above 13,000 feet, which is where most experienced Colorado climbers eventually end up when the fourteener trailheads get full.

Colorado has more climbing than any one person can work through in a lifetime, spread across granite, sandstone, limestone, gneiss and choss. What it does not have is an obvious on-ramp, because the sport is really four different sports wearing the same jacket.

Here is the ladder, bottom to top, with where to actually go.

Rung One: Bouldering

No rope. No partner strictly required. A crash pad, shoes, chalk, and problems that are usually under twenty feet tall.

Bouldering uses the V-scale, running from V0 at the easy end to V17 at the outer limit of what humans have done. V0 to V2 is where a fit beginner lives. The scale is open-ended and deliberately vague, which climbers complain about constantly and have never fixed.

Colorado's bouldering is world-class and unusually accessible:

  • The Flatirons and Boulder Canyon. The default. Minutes from town, thousands of problems, and the historical center of American bouldering.
  • Mount Blue Sky area. High-altitude granite that stays cool when the Front Range is baking. Renamed from Mount Evans in 2023.
  • Chaos Canyon, Rocky Mountain National Park. Alpine bouldering at 11,000 feet with a real approach. Short season, spectacular setting.
  • Poudre Canyon. Northern Colorado's quieter option, and less crowded on a Saturday than anything near Boulder.

Start indoors. Denver and the Front Range have a dense concentration of climbing gyms, and three months of gym bouldering will teach you movement that outdoor granite will otherwise beat out of you slowly and painfully.

Rung Two: Sport Climbing

Rope, harness, belayer, and bolts already drilled into the rock. You clip as you go. It is the safest way to climb hard.

Sport and trad both use the Yosemite Decimal System, where technical rock climbing is class 5 and the decimal that follows is the difficulty: 5.0 through 5.15d. Anything from 5.5 to 5.8 is beginner terrain. Most recreational climbers plateau somewhere in the 5.10 to 5.11 range and are perfectly happy there.

Rung Three: Trad

Traditional climbing means you place your own protection into cracks as you climb and remove it on the way out. Nothing is pre-installed. It requires judgment that cannot be rushed, and it is where Colorado's reputation was made.

  • Eldorado Canyon. Sandstone south of Boulder, and one of the most historically significant crags in the country. Notoriously sandbagged — a 5.9 in Eldo will humble somebody who climbs 5.10 in a gym.
  • Lumpy Ridge. Granite domes above Estes Park. Long, moderate, classic multi-pitch lines.
  • The Diamond on Longs Peak. A vertical big wall whose base sits above 13,000 feet. Serious alpine commitment, short season, genuine consequence.
  • Black Canyon of the Gunnison. Remote, loose in places, hard to retreat from, and generally regarded as one of the most serious trad venues in the lower 48. This is a destination for people who already know what they are doing.

Rung Four: The Fourteeners

This is the one everybody has heard of, and it is not technically climbing at all until it is.

Peaks are graded on the Yosemite class system, which measures the terrain rather than the difficulty of moves:

ClassWhat it meansColorado examples
1Walking on a maintained trailMount Elbert, Quandary Peak, Handies Peak
2Off-trail hiking, talus, occasional handsMount Bierstadt, Grays and Torreys
3Real scrambling. Hands required, exposure, route-findingLongs Peak via the Keyhole, Crestone Peak
4Steep scrambling where a fall is likely fatal. Many parties rope upCapitol Peak, Pyramid Peak, the Maroon Bells
5Technical roped climbingStandard routes on a handful of summits and most direct lines

The gap between class 2 and class 3 is enormous and it is where most people get into trouble. Class 2 is a hard hike. Class 3 is a place where a slip has consequences and where cloud cover can erase the route entirely.

Two peaks deserve a specific warning. The Maroon Bells are known as the Deadly Bells for a reason — the rock is rotten mudstone that pulls out in your hand, and the standard routes are far more dangerous than their grade suggests. Capitol Peak's Knife Edge is exactly what it sounds like. Neither is a reasonable early fourteener.

Start with Elbert, Quandary, Bierstadt or Grays. Read a current trip report from the last two weeks before you go, not a guidebook from 2015 — routes change, and so do trailhead parking rules.

The Access Rules People Keep Breaking

Colorado climbing access is not guaranteed. It is negotiated, constantly, and it gets revoked.

  • Raptor closures. From roughly February through July, sections of Eldorado Canyon, Boulder's open space cliffs and many other crags close for nesting peregrine falcons and golden eagles. These are legally enforced and dates shift year to year. Check before you drive.
  • Private land. Several fourteener approaches cross private property under voluntary agreements. Those agreements have been suspended before after trespassing and litter. If a sign says stay on the easement, stay on the easement.
  • Human waste. Alpine soil does not process anything. Above treeline, pack it out. Wag bags are stocked at many trailheads for free.
  • Parking. Popular trailheads now run shuttles, reservations or timed permits. Showing up at 6 a.m. and hoping is no longer a strategy at Quandary, Maroon Bells or much of Rocky Mountain National Park.

The Access Fund, which is headquartered in Boulder, exists specifically to keep these places open. It is worth the membership if you climb here more than a few times a year.

The Part Nobody Trains

Climbers train fingers and forearms. What limits most people on a Colorado alpine day is none of that.

It is cardiovascular capacity at 13,000 feet, hip and ankle stability on loose talus, and the ability to keep making good decisions when you are ten hours in and cooked. A ten-mile day with 4,500 feet of gain at altitude is an endurance event, and the fifteen-minute gym session that builds a stronger crimp does nothing for it.

The best off-season preparation I have seen for Colorado alpine days is not more climbing. It is combat sports conditioning — sustained interval work, real grip endurance, balance under fatigue, and the specific mental training of staying composed while your heart rate is pinned. Rounds on a bag translate to switchbacks better than almost anything else does.

06 · Culture · 1,850 words

The Other Colorado: Art, Science, Fossils And Dark Skies

Everything worth doing in this state that does not require boots — which turns out to be most of it.

ZW Zach WennstedtFounder & Editor-in-Chief · Raised in Boulder, Colorado

What is there to do in Colorado besides hiking?

A great deal. Colorado has an immersive art complex in Denver, a museum holding roughly 95% of one major American painter's life output, dinosaur tracks you can touch twenty minutes from downtown, an atmospheric research lab designed by I.M. Pei that is free and open to the public, and more certified Dark Sky places than anywhere else in the world.

Colorado's tourism image is a person in a puffy jacket on a summit. Fine. But the state also runs a serious cultural and scientific economy, and a rainy afternoon here is not a wasted day — it is an opportunity to see the parts of Colorado that the outdoor listicles have never once mentioned.

Denver, Which Is Not Just A Place You Fly Into

Meow Wolf Convergence Station is four floors of immersive art built by a collective of hundreds of artists, wrapped around a science fiction narrative you can either follow closely or completely ignore. It is genuinely unlike anything else, it is not a passive experience, and it takes most people three hours minimum. Buy tickets ahead.

The Clyfford Still Museum is the most unusual museum in the American West, and almost nobody outside the art world knows why. Still was one of the founding figures of Abstract Expressionism. He withdrew from the commercial art world and stipulated that his estate go, in its entirety, to any American city willing to build a permanent home for it. Denver did. The result is a single museum holding roughly ninety-five percent of one major artist's total life output — something that exists nowhere else for any painter of his stature.

The Denver Art Museum occupies two buildings that argue with each other. The 1971 Martin Building was designed by Italian architect Gio Ponti and is one of the first high-rise art museums ever built. The 2006 Hamilton Building is a Daniel Libeskind design of angular titanium planes that appears to be falling over. Inside, the Indigenous Arts of North America collection is among the most significant anywhere.

The Denver Museum of Nature & Science sits in City Park with a view of the entire Front Range from its west windows. Serious paleontology and anthropology collections, plus the Gates Planetarium. Bring kids or do not — it works either way.

Boulder's Federal Science, Which Is Open To You

Boulder holds one of the densest concentrations of federal atmospheric and physical science in the country, and a surprising amount of it is publicly accessible.

The NCAR Mesa Laboratory sits on a mesa above town in a building designed by I.M. Pei, who studied the cliff dwellings at Mesa Verde before drawing it. The concrete was mixed with local sandstone aggregate so the building would weather into the color of the Flatirons behind it. There is a free public exhibit floor on atmospheric science and climate, free parking, and trails leaving from the lot. It is one of the best free hours in Colorado.

Fiske Planetarium at the University of Colorado is among the largest planetariums in the United States and runs public shows and live star talks through the school year.

The physics done in Boulder's federal labs has produced multiple Nobel Prizes. If you have ever wondered why so many extremely specific atomic clocks live in one small Colorado town, that is the reason.

The Dinosaurs Are Closer Than You Think

Twenty minutes west of Denver, in Morrison, there is a road cut called Dinosaur Ridge where you can stand in front of the fossils rather than behind glass.

One side of the ridge exposes the Morrison Formation — Jurassic rock, roughly 150 million years old, containing bones of the animals everybody learned as a child. The formation is literally named after the town. On the other side, in younger Cretaceous rock, there are hundreds of dinosaur footprints preserved on a tilted slab, made when this was a coastal mudflat. You can walk the road for free or take a guided bus.

Further west, Dinosaur National Monument straddles the Colorado–Utah line and contains a quarry building constructed directly around a rock face holding roughly 1,500 fossil bones, left in place where they were found. You can put your hand on a 150-million-year-old femur.

Landscapes That Are Really Geology Lessons

Red Rocks Amphitheatre is a concert venue built between two 300-foot sandstone monoliths, and the acoustics are a geological accident rather than an engineering achievement. Free to walk during the day when nothing is scheduled, and the trail system around it is open year-round.

Garden of the Gods in Colorado Springs is free, always has been, and always will be — the land was donated to the city in 1909 on the explicit condition that it remain free to the public forever. Vertical red sandstone fins pushed upright by the same uplift that raised Pikes Peak.

Pikes Peak itself is reachable by cog railway, highway or a very long hike. In 1893 a schoolteacher named Katharine Lee Bates reached the summit, looked out, and wrote the words that became "America the Beautiful." The purple mountain majesties were these.

Great Sand Dunes National Park holds the tallest dunes in North America — roughly 750 feet — piled against the Sangre de Cristo range. Medano Creek runs seasonally at the base in a strange pulsing surge, though in a low-snow year like 2026 it may be thin or gone by early summer.

Mesa Verde protects roughly 5,000 archaeological sites, including some 600 cliff dwellings built by Ancestral Puebloan people. Cliff Palace requires a ranger-led ticketed tour and it is worth arranging in advance.

The Dark Sky Advantage

This is the category where Colorado genuinely leads the world, and almost nobody markets it.

Colorado is home to 13 International Dark Sky Parks and eight International Dark Sky Communities. More than 30 places statewide, including a dozen state parks, have formally begun the certification process — more than anywhere else on Earth. In late 2025 Breckenridge became the first mountain ski resort community in Colorado to earn Dark Sky Community status. Chimney Rock National Monument received a Dark Sky Park designation in December 2025, and Old Snowmass became the newest Dark Sky Community in February 2026.

New for 2026, the Colorado Tourism Office is launching a Colorado Stargazing Trail with itineraries and planning resources built specifically around night skies.

Practical advice for actually seeing anything:

  • Go near a new moon. A full moon washes out the Milky Way completely. This matters more than location.
  • Give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes. Dark adaptation is a real physiological process. One glance at a phone screen resets it.
  • Use red light only. Red preserves night vision. Most headlamps have a red mode nobody uses.
  • Dress far warmer than the daytime suggests. Standing still at 9,000 feet at midnight in September is a genuinely cold activity.
  • Check for smoke. In an active fire year, haze can ruin a clear forecast. The dry air that makes Colorado great for astronomy is the same dry air that makes it burn.

The Genuinely Odd Ones

Bishop Castle, near Rye in the Wet Mountains, is a full stone castle with towers, a dragon and iron catwalks, built almost entirely by one man — Jim Bishop — who started in 1969 and largely never stopped. There is no admission fee and there are no safety railings in the modern sense. It is a monument to stubbornness and it has to be seen.

The National Mining Hall of Fame and Museum in Leadville sits in the highest incorporated city in the country and tells the silver story properly, including the parts that ended badly.

The Royal Gorge Bridge near Cañon City hangs 956 feet above the Arkansas River and was for decades the highest suspension bridge in the world.

And Denver's RiNo district is essentially an outdoor mural gallery that repaints itself every year. Free, walkable, and different every time you go.

A Rainy Day Plan That Is Better Than The Sunny One

Here is the itinerary I give people when the forecast falls apart: Clyfford Still in the morning, walk across the plaza to the Denver Art Museum after lunch, drive twenty minutes to Dinosaur Ridge in the late afternoon when the light on the tracks is best, and finish at Red Rocks whether or not there is a show.

That is a full day, it is mostly indoors, it costs less than a lift ticket, and it covers Abstract Expressionism, Gio Ponti, the Jurassic and 300-foot sandstone monoliths.

Colorado is not only a place to be outside. It is a place that has been carefully collecting things for a hundred and fifty years, and a lot of them are indoors waiting for a bad weather day.

Common questions

Colorado, Answered Plainly

What is Colorado best known for?

Colorado is best known for the Rocky Mountains, its 58 peaks above 14,000 feet, and world-class skiing at resorts like Vail, Aspen, Breckenridge and Steamboat. It also has four national parks, 26 designated scenic byways, more than 300 days of sunshine in many areas, and the highest mean elevation of any U.S. state at roughly 6,800 feet.

How many 14ers are in Colorado?

There are 58 peaks above 14,000 feet in Colorado, according to the Colorado Geological Survey. The highest is Mount Elbert at 14,440 feet, which is the second-highest peak in the contiguous United States. Colorado also has roughly 647 peaks above 13,000 feet.

Why is Colorado called the Centennial State?

Colorado became the 38th state on August 1, 1876 — exactly 100 years after the signing of the Declaration of Independence. President Ulysses S. Grant signed the statehood proclamation, and the centennial timing gave the state its nickname. In 2026 Colorado marks 150 years of statehood in the same year the nation marks 250.

What is the best time of year to visit Colorado?

June through September is best for hiking, camping and scenic drives, though afternoon thunderstorms are near-daily from mid-July into early September. Mid-September through mid-October is best for aspen color, working from high elevation downward. December through March is ski season. May and early November are the quietest and cheapest, but many high mountain roads are still closed.

How long does it take to adjust to Colorado's altitude?

Most people adjust to Denver's 5,280 feet within 24 to 48 hours. Adjusting to mountain elevations above 9,000 feet takes longer — often two to three days. Sleep at a lower elevation for your first night, drink more water than usual, skip alcohol on arrival, and save your most demanding hike for day three. If you develop a headache with nausea that will not resolve, descend.

Do I need a reservation for Rocky Mountain National Park?

Yes, during the timed entry season. For 2026 the system runs from May 22 into mid-October, with two permit types: Timed Entry+ Bear Lake Road (5 a.m. to 6 p.m., ending October 18) and standard Timed Entry for the rest of the park (9 a.m. to 2 p.m.). Reservations are booked on Recreation.gov for a $2 processing fee. Additional slots are released at 7 p.m. Mountain Time the night before each arrival date.

Can I have a campfire in Colorado right now?

It depends entirely on current fire restrictions, which are set county by county and by each land management agency, and which change frequently. In dry years like 2026, expect Stage 1 or Stage 2 restrictions across much of the state. Always confirm with the county sheriff and the managing agency for the specific place you are camping, on the day you leave. When in doubt, cook on a liquid or gas stove.

When do the aspens change color in Colorado?

Colorado's aspens turn by elevation rather than by a single statewide date. High passes above 10,000 feet typically peak from mid to late September, mid-elevation areas between 8,000 and 9,500 feet peak in late September into early October, and Front Range foothills and city parks hold color into mid-October. Drought years push the whole sequence earlier and shorten it, which is the case in 2026.

What number do I call for Colorado road conditions?

Dial 511 from any cell phone or landline inside Colorado for statewide road conditions, closures and chain law information from the Colorado Department of Transportation. You can also use COtrip.org for a live map with traffic cameras. Note that 411 is directory assistance, not travel information.

Is Colorado in a drought in 2026?

Yes. Colorado ended the 2025–26 winter with statewide snowpack around 61% of median, the lowest reading in the modern snow telemetry record. Parts of Summit, Eagle, Pitkin, Lake and Park counties reached exceptional drought status, and much of northwest Colorado remained in extreme or severe drought. Expect fire restrictions, low river flows and an early aspen season.
