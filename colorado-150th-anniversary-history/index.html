Colorado At 150: What A Century And A Half Actually Built

Gold, silver, railroads, skis and satellites — and one word nobody else in America gets to use this year.

Why is Colorado called the Centennial State?

Colorado became the 38th state on August 1, 1876 — exactly 100 years after the Declaration of Independence. President Ulysses S. Grant signed the statehood proclamation, and the timing gave Colorado its nickname. That coincidence is also why 2026 is unusual: Colorado turns 150 in the same year the country turns 250.

History Colorado has a word for it. Sesquisemiquincentennial. It is not elegant and it will not fit on a t-shirt, but it is accurate, and Colorado is the only state in the country that gets to use it. Every other state's anniversary is just an anniversary. Ours is permanently stapled to the nation's, because of a hundred-year gap that a territorial legislature was very deliberately racing to hit.

So what did a century and a half actually produce? Here is the honest version, including the parts the tourism copy skips.

1858–1876: The Rush That Mostly Failed

Colorado begins, in the American sense, with a rumor. In 1858 prospectors found small amounts of gold near the mouth of Cherry Creek, where Denver sits now. The news traveled east and got wildly exaggerated on the way. An estimated hundred thousand people set out for the Rockies under the slogan "Pikes Peak or Bust."

Roughly half of them turned around. The gold was real but thin, and the ones who stayed mostly did not get rich. The people who did get rich sold them shovels, flour and lumber. That pattern — the boom enriching the supplier rather than the digger — repeats through the entire state's history, right up through the ski industry and the tech corridor.

Colorado Territory was organized in 1861. Statehood took another fifteen years and two failed attempts, and it finally arrived on August 1, 1876. The proclamation Grant signed is now held by History Colorado and was displayed publicly in Denver on the 150th anniversary this August.

1878–1893: Silver, And Then No Silver

The gold rush was the prologue. Silver was the fortune.

Leadville sits at 10,152 feet and is the highest incorporated city in the United States. In the late 1870s it became one of the richest square miles on the continent. The silver boom built opera houses, mansions, narrow-gauge railroads and an entire political class. Horace Tabor went from running a general store to serving in the U.S. Senate.

It ended almost overnight. In 1893 Congress repealed the Sherman Silver Purchase Act, the price of silver collapsed, and Colorado's economy went with it. Mines closed in weeks. Towns that had ten thousand residents had a few hundred within a decade. Most of Colorado's famous ghost towns are silver towns, not gold towns, and 1893 is the year that killed them.

The same year, something else happened that Colorado should be far better known for. In November 1893, Colorado's male voters approved women's suffrage by popular referendum. Wyoming had granted it earlier as a territory, but Colorado was the first state where men actually voted at the ballot box to extend the franchise to women — twenty-seven years before the Nineteenth Amendment.

1900–1945: Labor, Dust And A War Bargain

The mining economy that built Colorado also produced some of the ugliest labor conflict in American history. In April 1914, during a coal strike in Las Animas County, a confrontation between strikers and the Colorado National Guard ended with roughly two dozen people dead, including women and children who suffocated in a cellar beneath a burning tent colony. The Ludlow Massacre changed federal labor policy and it belongs in any honest telling of this state.

Then the plains. Eastern Colorado is not mountains, and in the 1930s the Dust Bowl hit Baca and Prowers counties as hard as anywhere in the country. Whole counties depopulated. The eastern third of Colorado has never fully recovered its population, and it remains the part of the state that visitors and marketers both routinely forget exists.

The turn came from the war. In 1942 the Army built Camp Hale near Leadville to train the 10th Mountain Division in alpine and ski warfare. Thousands of soldiers learned to ski at altitude in Colorado. When they came home, a remarkable number of them went into the ski business.

1946–1976: The Mountains Become A Product

Aspen reopened as a ski resort in 1946, backed by Walter Paepcke, who wanted a town that combined skiing with music, philosophy and design — an idea that sounded absurd and turned out to be worth billions.

Vail followed in 1962, founded by Pete Seibert and Earl Eaton, both 10th Mountain veterans. Within twenty years Colorado had converted its high country from a mining economy into a recreation economy. The mines had extracted metal. The resorts extracted altitude, snow and scenery, and unlike ore, those do not run out.

Then Colorado did something no place has done before or since. Denver was awarded the 1976 Winter Olympics. In 1972, Colorado voters passed a ballot measure barring the state from funding them. The Games were handed to Innsbruck. Colorado remains the only place ever to be awarded a modern Olympics and then turn them down.

That vote is the most Colorado thing in this entire article. It was driven by a coalition worried about growth, cost and environmental damage — the same argument the state is still having, fifty years later, in every mountain town council meeting.

1976–Now: Satellites, Labs And The Water Question

The modern Colorado economy is less photogenic and much larger. Boulder hosts a dense cluster of federal science — NOAA, NIST and NCAR among them — and the physics work done in those labs has produced multiple Nobel Prizes. Colorado Springs and the Front Range host a national aerospace and defense corridor that quietly employs tens of thousands.

And underneath all of it sits water. Colorado is a headwaters state. No major river flows into Colorado; they all start here and flow out. The Colorado River rises in Rocky Mountain National Park and ends up watering Phoenix, Los Angeles and the Imperial Valley. The 1922 Colorado River Compact divided that water among seven states based on flow measurements taken during an unusually wet stretch, and every water fight in the American West since has been an argument with that document.

Which is why a low snow year here is national news. See the water year piece below for what 2026 in particular is doing.

The Places That Got Protected

Colorado's four national parks were each created for a different reason, and together they are a decent summary of the state.

ParkEstablishedWhy it exists
Mesa Verde1906First national park created to protect the works of people rather than landscape — the Ancestral Puebloan cliff dwellings
Rocky Mountain1915Alpine tundra, the Continental Divide and the headwaters of the Colorado River
Black Canyon of the Gunnison1999A gorge so narrow and deep that parts of it get roughly half an hour of sunlight a day
Great Sand Dunes2004The tallest dunes in North America, sitting against 13,000-foot peaks

Great Sand Dunes was protected as a national monument in 1932 and redesignated a national park and preserve in 2004. Source: National Park Service.

What The 150th Actually Looks Like

The commemoration is running all year and it is deliberately decentralized. The signature program is Stories in the Sky, a series of roughly 150 community drone shows staged across the state from late 2025 through December 2026. History Colorado is also running a digital passport designed to route people into small towns and independent businesses rather than the same overloaded trailheads.

On Colorado Day this August, the Capitol hosted a reenactment of the 1876 statehood proclamation, a cake cutting, a square dance inside the building, and lowriders parked on the west lawn — which is a fairly complete portrait of the state in one photograph.

There was also a small, genuinely nerdy detail worth noting. The Mile High marker on the Capitol steps was updated during the ceremony, but only temporarily. The official elevation datum is being recalculated by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and will not be finalized until 2027. Denver's exact mile-high line is, at this moment, provisional.

One hundred and fifty years in, and we are still measuring the place.
