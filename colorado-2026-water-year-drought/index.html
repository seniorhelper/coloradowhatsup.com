The 2026 Water Year: Why A Dry Winter Is Still Shaping Your Trip

Snowpack hit 61% of median in February — the worst reading in the modern record. Eight months later, that number is still deciding what you can do here.

Is Colorado in a drought in 2026?

Yes. Colorado came out of the 2025–26 winter with statewide snowpack near 61% of median — ranked as the worst in a snow telemetry record that goes back to about 1987. Parts of Summit, Eagle, Pitkin, Lake and Park counties reached exceptional drought, the U.S. Drought Monitor's most severe category, and most of northwest Colorado sat in extreme or severe drought.

Almost nobody plans a Colorado trip around hydrology. You should, at least a little, because in this state the snow that fell in January is the reason a river is runnable in July, a campground has a fire ban in August, and the aspens turn a week and a half early in September.

Here is the chain, in plain terms, and where 2026 sits on it.

What A Water Year Even Is

Hydrologists do not use the calendar year. They use the water year, which runs October 1 through September 30. The logic is clean: the snow that starts falling in October is the water you will be drinking, floating and fighting fires with the following summer. Grouping October through September keeps one winter's snow with the summer it feeds.

The measurement that matters is not snow depth. It is snow water equivalent — how much actual water is locked in that snow. Two feet of dense wet snow holds far more water than four feet of the dry powder Colorado is famous for. A network of automated SNOTEL stations across the mountain West weighs the snowpack continuously and reports it as a percentage of the long-term median for that date.

That percentage is the single number that tells you what kind of summer Colorado is about to have.

What 2026 Read

Badly.

By mid-February the statewide figure was 61% of median. Officials noted that it would have taken record-breaking snowfall for the rest of the season just to reach a normal peak — and that did not happen. The state's fire agency said publicly that conditions in places looked worse than the winters that preceded 2012 and 2020, two of the most destructive fire years in Colorado history.

The effects showed up before the snow even melted. Frisco closed its Dillon Reservoir boat ramp for the entire 2026 season, on guidance that the reservoir would not fill enough to float the docks. That decision cost the town north of $300,000 in revenue, and it happened in February, based purely on what the snowpack was not doing.

The lack of snow also pushed fire risk earlier into the calendar and forced agencies to delay or cancel prescribed pile burns, which need snow on the ground to be conducted safely. That backlog of unburned fuel does not disappear. It waits.

Five Things It Changes For Visitors

1. Whitewater seasons compress

Colorado's rafting season is driven by runoff. In a good year, high water on the Arkansas, Colorado and Animas holds into early July and the big-water trips run for weeks. In a year like this, peak flow arrives earlier, drops faster, and the season for the high-consequence stretches can end in June.

That does not mean no rafting. It means outfitters shift to sections that run well at lower flow, and the trip you booked as a class IV may be running closer to class III. Call the outfitter and ask what the gauge is reading rather than what the brochure says. You can check the same gauges yourself — the U.S. Geological Survey publishes real-time streamflow for hundreds of Colorado sites, free.

2. Fishing gets restricted for the fish, not for you

Low flows mean warm water, and warm water holds less dissolved oxygen. When river temperatures climb into the upper 60s and low 70s, a trout that fights a long battle and gets released often dies anyway. Colorado Parks & Wildlife responds with voluntary fishing closures, usually asking anglers to stay off the water from late morning through evening.

These are voluntary. Honor them anyway. Fish early, fish the tailwaters below dams where the water comes out cold, and pack a thermometer. If it reads 70, go get breakfast.

3. Fire bans become the default

Expect restrictions, and expect them to change while you are already out there. The two tiers you will see most:

  • Stage 1 generally bans open fires outside permanent developed campground rings, plus fireworks and smoking outside a vehicle or cleared area. Liquid and gas stoves usually stay legal.
  • Stage 2 is far broader — typically no fires of any kind including charcoal, restrictions on chainsaws and welding, and limits on driving off established roads.

Bans are set county by county and agency by agency, so a single weekend trip can cross three different rule sets. Check each county you will be sleeping in, the day you leave.

4. Burn scars turn rain into flash floods

This is the one people underestimate. After a fire, soil can become hydrophobic — it sheds water instead of absorbing it. A short, ordinary thunderstorm over a burn scar can produce a debris flow in a drainage that has never flooded in living memory.

Colorado's 2025 season included the Lee Fire at roughly 137,758 acres, one of the largest in state history. Those scars will be generating flash flood risk for years. If a flash flood warning is issued for a burn area while you are camped in a canyon below it, leave. Do not wait to see water.

5. Fall color runs early and burns out faster

Drought-stressed aspens change sooner, hold less vivid color, and drop leaves faster than trees in a wet year. Guides that tell you to wait for the first week of October are repeating a normal-year rule.

For 2026, plan the high country — passes above 10,000 feet, the San Juans, Kebler Pass — for mid to late September. Mid-elevation spots like Kenosha and Guanella passes run late September into the first days of October. The Front Range and city parks hold latest, into mid-October. Color quality will vary sharply by microclimate: north-facing slopes and drainages with reliable water will look far better than dry exposed hillsides a mile away.

Why Colorado's Snow Is Everyone's Problem

Colorado is a headwaters state. Water flows out of here and essentially none flows in. The Colorado River starts in Rocky Mountain National Park and, by the time it is finished, has supplied cities and farmland across seven states and two countries.

The 1922 Colorado River Compact split that water using flow records from an unusually wet period, which means the river has been over-allocated on paper for most of a century. A 61% snow year in Colorado is therefore not a local inconvenience. It is a line item in reservoir operations from Lake Powell to the Imperial Valley.

The snow that falls on a pass in January is somebody's drinking water in July, in a city a thousand miles away. That is the actual reason Coloradans watch the SNOTEL numbers like a stock ticker.

The Honest Summary

2026 is a thin year. It does not mean cancel the trip. Colorado in a dry summer is still Colorado, and some of it is genuinely better — clearer skies for stargazing, firmer trails earlier in the season, and high routes that melt out weeks ahead of schedule.

It means shift the plan. Go earlier for water, later for color, and build in the assumption that you will be cooking on a stove rather than sitting around a fire. Check the county the morning you leave.

And if there is a burn scar above your campsite, look up before you unroll the sleeping bag.
